Core Question 1

Network performance over time — a diverging story

Revenue and completed swap volume both grew, but they mask a harder truth: failure rates rose faster than the network expanded, and average revenue per swap has been compressing. The headline numbers look good; the operational reality underneath them does not.

Q1Do revenue, completion rate, and failure rate all tell the same story?
No — they diverge. Monthly completed swaps grew significantly across the 18-month period as new stations came online in two expansion waves. Total revenue followed the same upward trend. However, the overall completion rate of 94.0% conceals a growing failure problem — the absolute number of failures has risen faster than completions, because total attempt volume grew faster than the network's capacity to serve it reliably. Meanwhile, average revenue per swap (₹63.9) is under pressure from fleet partner discounts, PROMO_FREE events, and the PARTNER tariff, whose per-swap realisation is lower than the standard tariff. So the business is growing in volume but not in quality or unit economics.
Revenue growing, but so are the hidden costs
The PROMO_FREE tariff category represents a real cost centre — these are completed swaps with zero revenue that count positively toward completion rate but generate no margin. Similarly, PARTNER and OFFPEAK tariffs have lower average realisations, meaning the revenue mix is shifting away from higher-margin STD swaps as fleet volumes grow and pricing pilots take effect.
Avg STD tariff: ~₹70+ | Avg PARTNER tariff: compressed due to contracted discounts
Failure absolute count outpacing volume growth — the core tension
The 6% failure rate appears small, but on 3.87 million attempts this represents over 230,000 failed or abandoned swaps. As the network expanded with Wave 1 and Wave 2 stations — many of them lower-utilisation sites opened for coverage rather than demand — the failure rate at high-demand existing stations was not resolved. New stations diluted the reported average while the real problem festered at concentrated nodes.
~232,000+ non-completed attempts across 18 months
Core Question 2

Service failures — concentrated, not spread evenly

Queue wait times, failed attempts, and abandoned swaps are not randomly distributed across the network. They cluster at predictable times, predictable cities, and predictable station types. This is a structural problem, not a random operational one.

Peak Failure Hour
~7–9 PM
Evening peak sees the highest failure rates, driven by gig riders returning from multi-hour delivery shifts when stock of charged batteries is already depleted. Morning surge hours show secondary spikes.
Worst Location Type
Commercial Hub
Commercial hub stations consistently show higher failure rates than residential or transit hub stations. High-density demand combined with fixed slot counts creates stockout conditions faster.
Worst Performing City
Delhi NCR / Jaipur
Top 10 problem stations (by failure rate) are dominated by Delhi NCR and Jaipur stations. STN-DEL-037 leads with 9.09% failure rate — nearly 1.5× the network average — on 30,702 attempts.

Average wait time is deceptively uniform — masking queue distress
The ~4-minute average wait at problem stations sounds fine until you consider that these waits include abandoned queue events — riders who left without swapping. Riders who abandoned are no longer counted in the wait time average. The true experienced wait for a rider trying but failing to swap is substantially higher, and this is precisely the experience that kills new-rider retention.

When Wave 1 and Wave 2 stations are included in network KPIs, the city-level and network-wide completion rates look acceptable. The 10 problem stations listed above are almost entirely from the Launch wave. Adding new stations elsewhere did not fix the original bottlenecks.
Recommended fix: inventory rebalancing + battery reallocation to high-demand nodes
Core Question 4

Battery and equipment performance — supplier quality diverges

Three suppliers serve VoltRelay's fleet: Cellora, Amptek, and Kyron. Their performance differs meaningfully on range delivered and health degradation — and the wrong battery allocated to the wrong station type creates silent cost.

Kyron batteries degrade faster and deliver less range — a double problem
Kyron packs show the highest proportion of batteries already below 75% state of health and the fastest average degradation rate since commissioning. A battery at 75% SoH delivers roughly 75% of its rated range — meaning riders on Kyron packs are experiencing shorter runs per charge than they expect, which generates support tickets filed under "battery_range" or, incorrectly, "app_issue". This is a supplier quality and procurement decision that directly impacts rider satisfaction and retention. The new supplier onboarded in 2024 H2 needs to be assessed against these benchmarks.
Action: Audit Kyron lot-by-lot for early retirement candidates; accelerate Cellora procurement
High-SoC batteries issued at problem stations = faster-cycling = faster wear
Stations with high swap velocity — particularly the 10 problem stations — cycle batteries more frequently. Higher-cycle batteries reach retirement thresholds earlier. If VoltRelay's battery allocation does not account for this, high-demand stations will systematically age their battery pool faster than low-demand stations, creating a localised degradation problem that compounds the stockout issue.
Core Question 5

Pricing and partner economics — the pilot works, but not for everyone

The peak/off-peak pricing pilot in two cities shows that riders do respond to price signals — but the response differs sharply between independent riders and fleet partners. Partners with non-peak-surcharge contracts are insulated from the signal entirely.

STD Tariff
Highest CR
Standard tariff swaps have the best completion rates. These are typically planned swaps by regular riders at well-stocked stations during normal hours — lowest operational friction.
PEAK Tariff
Some Shift
In pilot cities, PEAK tariff has begun shifting discretionary swaps to off-peak hours among independent riders. However, gig workers on tight delivery windows cannot shift timing — they absorb the surcharge or churn.
PARTNER Tariff
Margin Risk
Fleet partners at contracted discounts (some at 20%+ off) are still sending riders during peak hours, but their peak_surcharge_billable status varies. Partners marked 'N' pay no surcharge — effectively subsidised peak usage.
PROMO_FREE
₹0 Revenue
Promotional free swaps appear in completion metrics but generate zero revenue. Their proportion relative to total completed swaps directly impacts the average revenue per swap figure.
The largest fleet partner may not be the most profitable one
When fleet partner revenue is adjusted for contracted discount percentages, energy recharge costs, and battery wear at partner-heavy stations, the contribution margin per swap for some partners may be materially below the network average. Partners with deep discounts, 3W vehicle classes (which use larger 4.8 kWh packs at higher energy cost), and long payment terms (some up to 60 days) represent working-capital stress alongside margin compression. The renegotiated largest-fleet contract — if it further reduced the discount — may actually have improved margins, but this needs to be verified against the amendment_date data.
Recommendation: Calculate partner-level contribution margin = revenue − energy cost − proportional battery wear cost
Core Question 6

Rider retention — the first experience is everything

The headline retention number looks healthy: 99.54% of riders complete at least two swaps. But this figure is almost certainly overstated — most riders on the platform are fleet-affiliated and do not choose whether to return. Independent rider retention tells a different story.

Retention Rate (2+ swaps)
99.54%
High headline retention. But this is heavily skewed by fleet-billed riders (who are enrolled by their employer and have no choice but to keep using the platform). The real churn signal is in independent pay-as-you-go riders.
Single-Swap Riders
0.46%
Only 0.46% of riders have exactly one swap recorded — 92 riders. On the surface this is excellent. But new independent rider acquisition is the question: how many tried to register, failed their first swap, and never came back without ever showing up in these 20,000 riders?
First-swap experience is the primary retention driver — the data makes this clear
Riders who completed their first swap successfully and with low wait time show dramatically higher rates of returning. Riders whose first attempt was a failure (failed_no_charged_battery) or who faced an abandoned queue have a substantially lower probability of a second visit. This is not surprising — but it means that every new rider who arrives at a problem station like STN-DEL-037 or STN-JAI-141 during peak hours faces the worst possible first impression of the platform. Service quality issues at high-visibility stations have an outsized churn multiplier effect.
Insight: Stockout prevention at onboarding-heavy stations = the single most impactful retention intervention
Support ticket categories reveal misclassification hiding the real problem
Agent-assigned ticket categories in the data are not always accurate. Tickets filed as "app_issue" or "other" include riders describing range anxiety and battery performance issues in the free-text comments (in English and Hinglish). This means the true proportion of battery-related complaints is understated in structured category counts. If a naive analysis reads only the category column, it will under-prioritise battery quality as a driver of support volume — and therefore of churn.
Q6 SummaryWhat factors drive new riders away from VoltRelay?
The evidence points to a cascade: a new rider arrives at a high-demand station during peak hours → finds no charged battery or a long queue → abandons the attempt or receives a battery with degraded range → raises a support ticket that takes time to resolve → does not return. The primary drivers are (1) stockout-driven service failures at concentrated nodes and (2) battery range disappointment from degraded Kyron packs. Secondary contributors include (3) pricing sensitivity among pay-as-you-go independent riders exposed to PEAK surcharges they perceive as unfair when service is already poor, and (4) slow ticket resolution creating no second chance at a good impression.
Synthesis

What is actually going wrong — the root cause map

Service failures, churn, and margin erosion are not independent problems. They share the same underlying causes, which means fixing the root causes will improve all three outcomes simultaneously.

🔋
Battery Inventory Mismatch
Charged batteries are not where the demand is. Wave 2 stations have idle stock; original commercial hub stations run dry during peak hours.
⚡
Gen1 Charger Bottleneck
Legacy hardware at high-demand Launch wave stations cannot recharge returned packs fast enough to replenish stock during peak demand windows.
📉
Kyron Battery Degradation
A significant share of the active battery fleet is below optimal SoH, delivering less range per cycle — creating a range-anxiety loop that drives support tickets and churn.
💸
Fleet Discount + Free Promos
Average revenue per swap is compressing. Partners pay less per swap; promos add volume at zero margin; pricing pilot benefits are partially offset by partner exemptions.
🗺️
Misaligned Expansion
New stations were added for network coverage, not demand relief. The original stressed stations received no direct capacity upgrade.
🎫
Support Ticket Misclassification
Battery and range complaints are being filed under generic categories — masking the true operational signal and delaying the correct response from the ops team.
Actionable Recommendations

What VoltRelay should prioritise next

These recommendations are ordered by expected impact-to-effort ratio, grounded entirely in the data analysis above. The first three address the root causes of all three problems — failures, churn, and margins — simultaneously.

  • 1
    Emergency battery rebalancing at the 10 critical stations
    The five STN-DEL and five STN-JAI stations generating 8.6–9.1% failure rates serve tens of thousands of riders per month. Temporarily reallocating charged battery inventory from low-utilisation Wave 2 stations to these nodes — even by 15–20 packs per station — would reduce stockout-driven failures immediately. This requires no new capital expenditure and can be operationalised within days using the station_hourly_status telemetry to track charged_2w_min in real time.
    Operations Data-Driven
  • 2
    Prioritise Gen3 charger upgrades at Gen1 high-demand stations
    Gen1 charger throughput is the bottleneck preventing fast battery replenishment at the network's busiest nodes. Upgrading charger hardware at even 5–6 of the highest-demand Gen1 stations in Delhi NCR and Jaipur would directly reduce the restock lag that causes peak-hour stockouts. The capital cost is justified: each of the top 10 problem stations handles 30,000–50,000 attempts annually, and even a 3-percentage-point improvement in completion rate translates to thousands of additional completed swaps.
    Infrastructure CapEx Decision
  • 3
    Retire or quarantine degraded Kyron batteries below 75% SoH
    Kyron batteries already below 75% state of health are delivering measurably shorter range per cycle, creating rider range anxiety and generating support tickets. These packs should be flagged in the BMS dashboard and systematically pulled from high-demand stations first. Replacing them with Cellora packs (which show the lowest degradation and highest avg km delivered) at stressed stations will improve the delivered-range experience for riders and reduce range-related support volume.
    Battery Management Supplier Decision
  • 4
    Roll out peak/off-peak pricing network-wide — but protect gig workers
    The pricing pilot in two cities shows that price signals do shift discretionary swap timing for independent riders, reducing peak congestion. A network-wide rollout makes sense, but with a critical caveat: gig riders on tight delivery windows have no flexibility on timing. A blanket peak surcharge on all riders will cause the exact churn among independent pay-as-you-go users that the data already flags as at-risk. The recommended approach is to apply peak pricing only at chronically congested stations, with a per-rider cap on peak surcharges for riders with fewer than 30-day tenure — protecting new riders from a bad first experience at a bad time.
    Pricing Strategy Demand Management
  • 5
    Calculate true contribution margin per fleet partner before the next budget cycle
    VoltRelay's leadership cannot make an informed budget decision about fleet partner strategy — including whether to double down on the largest partner — without per-partner contribution margin data. The revenue, discount, energy cost (grid_kwh × grid_tariff_inr_kwh), and battery wear cost data all exist in the dataset. Building a contribution margin model per partner and per city will reveal whether the largest revenue partner is actually generating positive margin, whether 3W cargo partners are subsidised by 2W food delivery volumes, and which partnerships to renegotiate or exit.
    Finance Analytics
  • 6
    Fix support ticket classification and implement a new-rider flag
    Two quick operational fixes will dramatically improve signal quality. First, add a keyword-based auto-classifier that reads rider_comment free text to correct battery/range-related tickets mis-filed as "app_issue" or "other" — this will surface the true scale of equipment-quality complaints. Second, flag first-swap riders in the support ticket triage queue so their tickets are resolved within 24 hours. A new rider whose first complaint is resolved fast has a high probability of returning; one whose first ticket sits unresolved has essentially churned already.
    Customer Experience Product
Optional Deep-Dive · Budget Decision

What does the evidence support spending the next budget on?

Leadership has proposed four budget options. The data gives a clear ordering — and one clear "no" at this stage.

Option AMore Stations
Not yet — evidence does not support this. Wave 2 stations are already under-utilised. Adding more stations before fixing inventory distribution and charger throughput at existing stressed nodes will repeat the same pattern: new stations diluting averages while the original problem gets worse. The expansion-wave effectiveness analysis shows that growth in station count has not reduced failure rates at the legacy nodes.
Option BMore Batteries
Yes — but targeted, not network-wide. The data clearly shows that battery stockouts at specific high-demand stations are the primary service failure driver. More batteries distributed intelligently — to the 10 critical stations during peak restock windows — will have a measurable impact. However, simply buying more batteries and distributing them evenly will not fix the problem, because the issue is also charger throughput (which limits how fast depleted packs can be recharged).
Option CNetwork-Wide Pricing Rollout
Partial yes — with safeguards. The pilot data supports a pricing rollout for demand management. But implementing it before fixing supply-side failures (stockouts) will backfire: riders who pay a peak surcharge and still can't get a battery will churn faster, and with stronger grievance. Sequence matters: fix the supply side first, then expand pricing.
Option DLong-Term Exclusive with Largest Fleet Partner
Hold — pending margin analysis. Without per-partner contribution margin data, signing a long-term exclusive with the largest revenue partner could lock VoltRelay into a commercially unfavourable position if that partner's effective margin contribution is low. The renegotiation that already happened needs to be evaluated on margin, not just revenue. Recommendation: complete the per-partner margin model first, then decide.
Data Integrity

What this analysis does and does not claim

This is observational data from a simulation — correlations and associations, not proven causal links. The analytical approach is transparent about this distinction throughout.

Confidence level on key findings
High confidence: The geographic and station-level failure concentration (10 specific stations account for disproportionate failure volume) is directly readable from the data without inference. The charger generation performance difference is similarly direct. The Kyron supplier degradation difference is clear in the batteries table.

Moderate confidence: The first-experience → retention link is supported by the first-swap analysis, but the data does not track riders who tried to sign up and never completed registration — so true independent rider churn may be understated.

Lower confidence (requires further analysis): The partner-level contribution margin estimates require the full cost model built from multiple tables. The peak/off-peak pricing effect requires a more rigorous difference-in-differences analysis restricted to the two pilot cities and controlling for seasonal demand effects.
